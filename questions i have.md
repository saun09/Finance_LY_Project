# Questions I Have

## How is the risk score calculated? Are there two scores?

Yes — the system computes two independent scores and combines them with a strict rule (not a blend). The first is a **"stated" score** from the risk questionnaire itself: `compute_stated_tier()` in `backend/app/services/risk_profile.py` takes the user's questionnaire answers, applies a weighted sum (points per answer × question weight), and maps the total to a tier (1–5) using breakpoints defined in `backend/app/services/risk_profile_config.py`. This represents the person's self-reported *willingness* to take risk (what they think they can afford/handle). The second is an **objective "capacity ceiling"** from real financial data — `compute_capacity_ceiling()` looks at actual buffer/emergency fund coverage, EMI-to-income ratio, income stability, and insurance adequacy (all pulled from onboarding and spending data via `gather_capacity_inputs()` in `risk_profile_service.py`), and takes the *minimum* tier across those components. This represents the person's actual *ability* to bear risk, based on what their real numbers say they can afford — not their perception. Finally, `compute_final_tier()` combines them as `final_tier = min(stated_tier, capacity_ceiling)` — i.e. "ability before willingness": your actual financial capacity can only cap your risk tier downward, never be overridden by what you self-report on the questionnaire. If capacity caps the result, the system also returns which constraint caused it (e.g. low buffer, high EMI ratio) and "unlock conditions" telling the user what to change to raise their tier.

## Are the questionnaire's points/weights/breakpoints backed by any data?

No — checked `backend/app/services/risk_profile_config.py` directly, and these values are hand-designed, not empirically calibrated. Every question uses a plain 1–5 Likert scale where the safest answer is always 1 and the riskiest is always 5, evenly spaced — an ordering convention, not a measured value. The per-question weights (2, 3, or 4) reflect the developer's own judgment about which questions should matter more (e.g. `horizon` and `drawdown_reaction` are weighted 3, `experience` and `goal` are weighted 2), with no study or dataset behind the choice. The tier breakpoints (e.g. `(18, 26, 34, 42)`) are explicitly just an equal-width split of the total possible score range into 5 tiers, per the code's own comment — not derived from any distribution of real user scores or outcome validation. The one partial exception is the V2 question `sure_gain_tradeoff`, whose structure (guaranteed amount vs. escalating gamble) is explicitly commented as inspired by Grable & Lytton's (1999) published risk-tolerance research instrument — though its point values still follow the same linear 1–5 scale as every other question, not Grable & Lytton's actual scoring. So the design is internally consistent and documented, but not empirically validated (no backtest, no user study, no reliability/validity testing like Cronbach's alpha) — worth flagging as a limitation if this needs to hold up academically.

## How do I validate/defend the questionnaire's numbers for my dissertation?

Recommended validation plan, roughly in order of effort-to-payoff: (1) **Content validity** — have 3-5 independent reviewers (supervisor, peers) rate each question's relevance and guess the intended risk ordering of its options, without seeing the point values; agreement gives a citable Content Validity Index. (2) **Map every item to literature** — even self-written items should be framed as operationalizing an established construct (time horizon, loss aversion, experience, goal orientation are all standard dimensions in Grable & Lytton, FinaMetrica, and the Survey of Consumer Finances); state explicitly in the writeup which items are adapted vs. original. (3) **Pilot test + Cronbach's alpha** — run the questionnaire on ~20-30 people and compute Cronbach's alpha for internal consistency (target > 0.7); this is the standard reliability check expected in any psychometric section. (4) **Convergent validity** — in the same pilot, also administer the public-domain 13-item Grable & Lytton scale and correlate its total score against the custom score (Pearson r); a moderate-strong correlation supports that the custom instrument measures the same underlying trait. (5) **Known-groups validation** — show the score distinguishes two groups expected a priori to differ in risk tolerance (e.g. dependents/loans vs. none).

On the weights: hand-picked integers (2/3/4) are hard to defend as-is. A stronger, citable alternative is **unit weighting (all weights = 1)** per Dawes (1979), "The robust beauty of improper linear models in decision making" — a classic finding that equal weights perform nearly as well as statistically-derived weights absent a calibration sample large enough to justify differential weighting. If differential weights are still wanted, derive them from pilot data (item-total correlations or factor analysis) rather than guessing. On tier breakpoints: prefer setting them at the quartiles of the pilot sample's actual score distribution rather than equal-width slices of the theoretical range, so tiers reflect real score spread rather than an assumption of uniform distribution. If a full pilot isn't feasible in the project timeline, an explicit "Threats to Validity / Limitations" section naming these gaps and proposing them as future work is an accepted fallback.
